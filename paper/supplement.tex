\section{Complete specification of the STEAM algorithm}
\label{ssect:steam algorithm spec}

This section specifies the STEAM algorithm at the level of implementation
detail.
The physical construction and its motivation are described in the main
text and its appendices, while the intent here is that the simulation could be
reproduced exactly from this description together with the main-text
equations. The reference implementation is the \texttt{turbulon-model}
repository, and the full analysis for this paper is contained in the
companion \texttt{turbulon-analysis} repository.

The inputs are listed in Table \ref{tab:steam inputs}.

The implied vertical outer scale $k_{z,L}(z) = l_s(z)\left(L/l_s(z)\right)^{H_z}$ must be smaller than the domain height at every level.

Logarithmically spaced size classes are defined as $k_i = L\, 2^{-i/n_c}$
for $i = 0, 1, \dots, n$, proceeding from the outer scale down to the
class nearest $k = 2dx$, the smallest turbulon that is Nyquist-sampled
by the output grid: $n = \mathrm{round}[n_c\log_2(L/(2dx))]$. When the
outer scale is a power-of-two multiple of $dx$, the conventional
configuration, the finest class is $2dx$ exactly. For each $k_i$ the vertical turbulon size is $k_{z,i}(z) =
l_s(z)(k_i / l_s(z))^{H_z}$, evaluated with the local spheroscale;
where the piecewise-isotropic option is selected, $k_{z,i} = k_i$ for
$k_i < l_s(z)$. The list of size classes does not depend on the
sparsity factors: the cascade always terminates at the class nearest
$k = 2dx$. The sparsity factors
instead set how finely each class is resolved on its own working grid
(next section): at $s = 1$ the finest class works directly on the
output grid, while for $s > 1$ it is computed on a grid finer than the
output and restricted to the output grid afterward.

\subsection{Normalization}

The outer-scale amplitude profile $C_{\Phi, L}(z)$ is computed for each
scalar $\Phi \in \{h, q_t\}$ from the mean profile as described in
Appendix A. At each profile level, the Haar fluctuation at the local
vertical outer scale is computed by taking the mean of the profile over the upper half of
a $k_{z,L}(z)$-wide window minus the mean over the lower half. The
profile is read as the piecewise-linear function its samples define,
and the two half-window means are its exact integrals over
$[z, z + k_{z,L}/2]$ and $[z - k_{z,L}/2, z]$. Beyond its ends the
function is extended by its end values. The windows are therefore not
rounded to whole profile cells, which would otherwise bias the
response high wherever a half-window spans only a few cells. The result is multiplied by the calibration
constant $\lambda$ and taken in absolute value.

$\lambda$ is set by the consistency requirement of Appendix A: the
vertical Haar fluctuation of the simulated fields at the vertical outer
scale must equal that of the mean profile. It is calibrated by running
the full algorithm on linear mean profiles for both scalars, on a domain
whose vertical extent contains one vertical outer scale. The mean
absolute vertical Haar fluctuation of the simulated columns is computed
at logarithmically spaced lags. A line of fixed slope $H_v = H_h/H_z$ is
anchored to the fluctuation at the second-smallest lag (the smallest,
at the grid spacing, is affected by discretization) and extrapolated to
$k_{z,L}$. The criterion is evaluated on this extrapolated line rather
than on the fluctuations measured at $k_{z,L}$ itself, because near the
outer scale the measured fluctuations mix the cascade with the profile,
and the criterion concerns the cascade amplitude alone. The ratio of
the profile's Haar fluctuation at $k_{z,L}$ to the extrapolated value,
averaged geometrically over the two scalars, multiplies $\lambda$, and
the calibration is repeated until the ratio converges to one. The
iteration is needed because the bound projection makes the response to
$\lambda$ slightly sublinear, so a single pass under-corrects. Because
the same Haar operator is applied to the profile and to the simulated
field, the envelope amplitude convention and the reference-resolution
convention of the interpolation-compensation factors below are absorbed
into $\lambda$. The fluctuation amplitude the cascade realizes from a
given $C_{\Phi,L}$ grows systematically with the horizontal Hurst
exponent, so $\lambda$ is calibrated at the production $H_h$ and does
not transfer across it; for $H_h = 0.45$, $\lambda = 0.28927$. The scale-dependent amplitudes
follow $C_{\Phi,k}(z) = n_c^{-1/\alpha}\, C_{\Phi,L}(z)(k/L)^{H_h}$
as described in Appendix A and are interpolated
to each size class's vertical grid.

The bound buffer widths are computed from the same amplitude profiles
as
\begin{align}
  b_{\Phi,k}(z) = n_b\, \frac{C_{\Phi,k}(z)}{1 - 2^{-H_h/n_c}},
\end{align}
the geometric sum, in closed form, of the mean absolute amplitudes of
the current class and of every class the cascade could ever add below
it. The sum deliberately extends past the classes a particular run
carries. Truncating it at a run's own finest class would make the
buffer, and with it the realized field, depend on where the run stops,
breaking the identity between a nested refinement and a deeper root
run (Sect.~\ref{sect:nested refinement}).

\subsection{Working grids}

For computational efficiency, each size class works on its own grid
with $\Delta x_k = k/(2s_x)$ and $\Delta y_k = k/(2s_y)$ --- coarser
than the output grid for all but the finest classes. When $s = 1$, the
spacing is half the turbulon size and the turbulon is represented
coarsely, as shown in the discretized-turbulon figure of the main-text
appendix.
The horizontal dimensions $n_{x,k} = L_x/\Delta x_k$ and $n_{y,k} =
L_y/\Delta y_k$ are exact integers provided $L_x$ and $L_y$ are integer
multiples of $L$. A domain axis narrower than the outer scale (the
strip nests of Sect.~\ref{ssect:strip diagnostics}) is also admitted.
Grid counts along it are rounded to at least one cell, and any turbulon
envelope wider than the extent is periodically folded onto it.

The vertical grid requires more care because $k_z(z)$ varies with
height. To compute $\Delta z$, cells are accumulated upward from the surface with local
spacing $k_z(k, l_s(z))/(2s_z)$ until the accumulated grid first reaches
or overshoots the domain top, with the overshooting cell retained. The resulting spacings are then rescaled by a single common factor
$\le 1$ so that they tile $L_z$ exactly. For a constant spheroscale this reduces
to a uniform grid at least as fine as the Nyquist target.

As described below, at each cascade step the fields are trilinearly interpolated to the next working grid dimensions. Neither the coarse sampling nor the interpolation preserves the mean absolute value of the turbulon perturbations: a size class that is poorly resolved on the output grid introduces more variability than its $C_{\Phi,k}$ factor alone would imply. To account for this, each class carries a compensation factor $\digamma$ that depends on $k/\Delta x$, the turbulon size in units of the output grid spacing (Table \ref{tab:interpolation compensation}). The factor is applied when the \emph{output} is composed, never inside the cascade itself. The cascade's running state carries no compensation, so it is the same field however deep the run goes; this matters because the gradient weighting and the bound factor read the running state, and a nested refinement continues from it. Instead, the cascade accumulates a compensation deficit field for each scalar, $\sum_i (\digamma_i - 1)\,\Delta_i$, where $\Delta_i$ is the increment class $i$ actually added. The deficit is interpolated between classes together with the state, and the output is the sum of the two (final-fields section below). The flux accumulates a deficit in the same way, from the change in its state across each class's advance. The retentions underlying $\digamma$ are measured by rerunning a single size class of the full algorithm at successively finer output resolutions and taking ratios of the resulting mean absolute perturbations.

Two effects contribute to the resolution dependence. First, at small $k/\Delta x$ the sampled points on the envelope curve land preferentially near the envelope peaks; at the extreme $s = 1$ the grid points coincide with the turbulon centers. A marginally resolved class therefore reads up to $1/0.337 \approx 3\times$ the amplitude of a well-resolved one. Second, at large $k/\Delta x$, each regrid resamples the piecewise-linear field at points that do not exactly coincide with the previous ones, especially in the vertical direction where upsampling occurs by a non-integer factor $2^{H_z}$. This removes 1--3\% of the amplitude per octave out to at least $k/\Delta x = 256$. There is consequently no fully converged reference resolution. The factors in Table \ref{tab:interpolation compensation} are instead normalized to $k/\Delta x = 512$, the largest class used by any simulation in this work. The choice of reference is a single overall constant and is absorbed into the calibration factor $\lambda$ above.

The compensation factors $\digamma$ are built from per-regrid ``retentions'', defined as the fraction of the mean absolute amplitude that survives one interpolation step. Retentions differ between the isotropic and anisotropic regimes because of the different vertical upsampling factors ($2$ versus $2^{H_z}$ per step), and were therefore measured separately in the two regimes (Table \ref{tab:hop retention}). A class's compensation factor is the delivery of the reference chain divided by the product of the retentions of the regrids that its perturbation field will undergo, each taken from the regime of that regrid's destination grid at each height level. A cascade crossing the spheroscale therefore composes isotropic retentions below it and anisotropic retentions above it, with the crossing step approximated by the regime of its destination grid.

The specific rule is as follows. Write $r(y)$ for the retention of one regrid, with $y$ the class's value of $k/\Delta x$ on the grid the regrid starts from. A class's perturbation field is created at $y = 2$, on the class's own working grid, and $y$ doubles at each subsequent regrid. Intermediate $y$ (which arise for $n_c > 1$) are interpolated linearly in $\log_2 y$. A regrid at $y$ at or beyond twice the last tabulated value retains 1, the class being well resolved by then; between the last tabulated value and twice it, the last tabulated retention applies. At each level $z$, a class whose perturbation field has yet to traverse regrids $m = 1, 2, \dots$ carries
\begin{align}
  \digamma(z) = D_{512} \Big/ \prod_m r_{\rho_m(z)}(y_m), \qquad
  D_{512} = \prod_{y = 2, 4, \dots, 256} r_{\mathrm{ani}}(y) \approx 0.337,
\end{align}
where $\rho_m(z)$ selects the column of Table \ref{tab:hop retention} by the regime of the $m$-th regrid's destination grid at that level, and $D_{512}$ is the delivery of the reference chain. The regrids reduce the class's perturbation field by exactly the product being divided by, so, once the deficit is added at the output, every class arrives with the same effective normalization $D_{512}$. For a run with no classes below the spheroscale this reduces to Table \ref{tab:interpolation compensation}.

\subsection{Cascade loop}

The perturbation fields $\widetilde{h}$ and $\widetilde{q_t}$ are
initialized to zero and the flux field $\mathcal{F}$ to one on the
coarsest grid. The algorithm then iterates over size classes from large
to small, performing the following sequence of operations at each class.

First, the perturbation and flux fields are trilinearly interpolated
from the previous class's grid to the current one, together with the
compensation deficits of the working-grids section once they exist,
and the mean profiles are linearly interpolated to the current
vertical grid. The deficits pass through the same regrids as the
states, since those regrids are exactly what they compensate.

The flux is then advanced by one size class. A sparse field $\gamma$ of
independent extremal ($\beta = -1$) L\'evy $\alpha$-stable variables,
$\alpha = 1.8$, is drawn at the turbulon centers every $s_x, s_y, s_z$
cells, i.e. every $k/2$ in physical space. No centers are placed within
$m\, k_z(k)$ of the surface or of the domain top ($m$ of
Table \ref{tab:steam inputs}, default 1). This noise is scaled by
$c_\mathcal{F} = c\, n_c^{-1/\alpha}$ and shifted by the deterministic
constant $c_\mathcal{F}^\alpha/(\alpha-1)$, which enforces $\langle
e^\gamma \rangle = 1$ exactly: writing $\gamma_0$ for the unscaled
draw, $\ln\langle e^{c_\mathcal{F}\gamma_0} \rangle =
c_\mathcal{F}^\alpha/(\alpha-1)$ in closed form, the $\alpha$-stable
generalization of the lognormal $-\sigma^2/2$. The expectation is
finite because $\beta = -1$ places the heavy tail on the side the
exponential suppresses. The flux is updated additively as
$\mathcal{F} \mathrel{+}= \mathfrak{T}_k * [(e^\gamma - 1)\mathcal{F}]$. After the addition, any negative values are set to zero. The clipped flux is then rescaled by a multiplicative factor that restores the volume
mean the field had before the perturbation was added. Level means are not
individually constrained. The flux's compensation deficit accumulates
the change in the field across this whole update, convolution, clip,
and mean restoration included; the field itself carries no
compensation.
The signed scalar noise $S_k$ is defined as the increment $(e^\gamma -
1)\mathcal{F}$, evaluated with the entering flux and normalized by the mean
absolute value of $e^\gamma - 1$ over the turbulon centers. The scalar noise is shared
between $h$ and $q_t$.


Next, the scalar amplitudes are computed from the noise, bound factor, and gradient weighing factor. The bound factor $g_\Phi$ is evaluated pointwise from the running field $f = \langle \Phi \rangle_t + \widetilde{\Phi}$ using the buffer width $b_{\Phi,k}(z)$ defined in Appendix A.
 
Gradients are taken with periodic
central differences in $x$ and $y$ and with one-sided differences at the
vertical boundaries (central in the interior). The gradient weight is computed as $W_{\Phi,k} =
|\nabla_h f| + (k_z/k)|\partial f/\partial z|$. The overall weighing factor $g_\Phi W_{\Phi,k} S_k$ is divided
by its mean absolute value over the turbulon centers at each level.
Finally, the normalized product
is multiplied by $C_{\Phi,k}(z)$, giving the amplitude field
$A_\Phi$.

The amplitude fields are then convolved with the turbulon envelope. The 3D
Mexican hat defined in Appendix A is constructed
with $\sigma = k/\pi$ isotropically in grid-index space and truncated at a distance $3k$ from its center along each
axis. If the kernel exceeds
the field extent horizontally it is periodically folded onto the field
period. $A_\Phi$ is convolved with this kernel using a horizontally periodic and vertically zero-padded convolution to obtain a candidate perturbation field for
$\widetilde{\Phi}_k$.

Because the candidate perturbation field may cause the running sum $\sum_{k\ge k_i} \widetilde{\Phi_k}$ to exceed its bounds, it cannot be straightforwardly added to $\sum_{k > k_i} \widetilde{\Phi_k}$. Instead, 
a bounded,
amplitude-preserving projection is used. At every level the added perturbation
must satisfy three conditions: (i) zero horizontal mean; (ii) mean
absolute amplitude as close as possible to that of the unclipped
candidate field, so that the amplitude at each scale is set by the
normalization above and the bounding neither enforces it a second
time nor erodes it; and (iii) $\Phi_{\min} \le \Phi \le
\Phi_{\max}$ pointwise. These criteria are met iteratively.
The added perturbation is taken from a two-parameter family: the
candidate field $\delta_0$ is scaled by $s$, shifted by $\mu$, and
clipped to the bounds,
\begin{align}
  \delta(s,\mu) = \min\!\big(\max(s\,\delta_0 - \mu,\; \Phi_{\min} -
  \Phi),\; \Phi_{\max} - \Phi\big).
\end{align}
The shift enforces condition (i). At fixed $s$, the level mean of
$\delta$ is a continuous, piecewise-linear, and non-increasing function
of $\mu$, so the value of $\mu$ that produces a zero mean is unique and
is found by bisection. The scale enforces condition (ii): the mean
absolute value of $\delta$ increases monotonically with $s$, and a few
multiplicative updates bring it to the target, each followed by
subtracting the level mean and re-clipping. The bisection for $\mu$
is performed once, at the end, to remove whatever mean this simple
iteration leaves. The simple iteration cannot serve as the final step
itself: its convergence rate is set by the fraction of clipped points,
and it makes no progress on levels held at a bound.

The three conditions cannot always be met at once. A perturbation that
has zero mean and keeps $\Phi$ within its bounds can have mean absolute
amplitude no larger than $2\min(\langle\Phi\rangle - \Phi_{\min},\;
\Phi_{\max} - \langle\Phi\rangle)$, because positive and negative
excursions must balance and the smaller of the two headrooms limits
both. The target amplitude exceeds this limit wherever the level mean
sits too close to a bound. For $q_t$ this happens at dry upper levels,
where the summed amplitudes $\sum_k C_{q_t,k}$ can reach ten times the
level mean. On such levels the iteration stops at the largest amplitude
the bounds permit, and conditions (i) and (iii) still hold exactly.
Once accepted, the perturbation is added to the running field, so
subsequent size classes see the gradients of the bounded field.

\subsection{Final fields and output files}

After the finest class, the outputs are composed from the cascade
states and their compensation deficits. Each scalar output is $\Phi =
\langle \Phi \rangle_t + \widetilde{\Phi} + \sum_i (\digamma_i - 1)
\Delta_{\Phi,i}$, projected onto $[\Phi_{\min}, \Phi_{\max}]$ with each
level's horizontal mean preserved. The projection is needed because
restoring amplitude to under-resolved classes can push the sum past
bounds that the per-class bounded projection held the state inside.
Each violating level is replaced by the closest in-bounds field with
the same horizontal mean, using the same shift-before-clip
construction as the cascade loop's bounded projection. The flux output is the flux field plus its deficit, clipped
at zero and rescaled to the volume mean of the uncompensated field,
the same correction each class applies. The composition is the last
operation and touches nothing the cascade or a descendant nest reads;
a run whose classes are all well resolved ($\digamma \equiv 1$) skips
it entirely. Temperature, water phases, and pressure are then
recovered from the composed $h$ and $q_t$ by the saturation adjustment
of the main-text appendix, in a separate pass.

Each simulation produces one NetCDF4 file, with the contents listed in
Table \ref{tab:output file}. The three-dimensional fields are single
precision. The input $z$ and spheroscale profiles are kept in double
precision: a nest rebuilds its grids from them, and a single-precision
round trip would move every class's spacing by a rounding step.
Compression is optional (off by default): when enabled,
the fields use the blosc-zstd filter (byte-shuffled, compression level
1), and chunks smaller than 16\,KiB are stored uncompressed. The
inputs are stored alongside the outputs so that every file is
self-describing, and scalar attributes record the full configuration:
grid dimensions and spacings, outer scale, domain height, bounds,
sparsity factors, cascade density, surface pressure, random seed,
$H_h$, $H_z$, $\lambda$, the flux parameters $c$ and $\alpha$, and
the envelope and anisotropy options.

Nested refinements (next section) are stored as NetCDF groups inside
the parent's file (e.g. \texttt{refinements/r0}) with the same layout.
A nest's coordinates are world-absolute, and attributes record its
parent group, its index range within the parent grid, and whether each
of its horizontal axes is periodic. A nest whose base is elevated
above the ground additionally stores the two-dimensional pressure
field at its base, taken from the parent's three-dimensional pressure,
from which its hydrostatic integration starts.

\subsection{Nested refinement}\label{sect:nested refinement}

A completed simulation can be ``refined'' to finer resolution over a portion of its domain by simply continuing the cascade within the desired region. The design requirement is that a nest be the same cascade carried further: identical, cell for cell, to a root run taken directly to the nest's resolution (\texttt{tests/heavy/test\_nest\_identity.py} verifies this bit-exactly). The nest therefore continues from the stored cascade states of Table \ref{tab:output file}, extracted over the nest region and interpolated trilinearly onto the nest's grids; a parent intended for refinement must store them, because the written fields are composed and projected and cannot be turned back into the states. The
amplitude profiles $C_{\Phi,k}$ are re-derived from the mean profile
by exactly the computation the root performed, with the root's outer
scale, $H_h$, and $\lambda$. Because the Haar response is evaluated
exactly on the profile's own grid (normalization section), this
reproduces the root's amplitudes class by class, with no discontinuity
at the overlap scale. The
flux anomaly requires no special handling: because the per-class
renormalization restores the volume mean the field entered with, a
nest keeps the regional flux anomaly it inherited.

The interpolation-compensation factors depend on the output grid, so
the deficit belonging to the nest's output differs from the parent's.
The parent therefore stores each class's added increments ($h$, $q_t$,
and flux) on that class's own working grid, in the
\texttt{class\_increments} group of Table \ref{tab:output file}; the
working grids form a geometric pyramid, so the storage is about
$1.14\times$ one output-grid field per stored field. From the stored
increments, the nest accumulates the deficit of its inherited classes
with $\digamma_j$ evaluated on its own output grid, $\sum_j
(\digamma_j - 1) \Delta_j$, through the same sequence of regrids the
cascade itself uses. Its cascade then continues the accumulation over
the nest's own classes, and the composition at the nest's output is
exactly a root's, because the composition is linear in the increments.
Without the stored increments, the parent's finest class would arrive
a factor $\digamma(16)/\digamma(2) \approx 2.7$ too weak in a nest
three octaves finer. A nest stores the full set of classes, the
inherited ones cut to its extent followed by its own, so that a nest
of a nest inherits in exactly the same form as a nest of a root.

The extraction carries a halo on each non-spanning horizontal side,
of per-class physical width equal to the turbulon support at that
class, so that turbulons centered outside the nest still reach into it.
The halo provides context only. It is excluded from every statistic
evaluated over the simulated domain --- the advective-weight
normalization, the mean absolute multiplier noise, the flux volume
means, and the bounded projection --- and it is discarded on output.
Bounds are still enforced across the halo, because the next class
reads its gradients and its bound factor. Along any periodic axis the
nest spans entirely, the parent's periodicity is
preserved with the parent extent as the period, and no halo is used;
a nest that spans, or whose halo would cross, a non-periodic parent
boundary is rejected. The vertical is always aperiodic: the halo is
clipped at the ground and domain top, and turbulon centers may lie
anywhere between the domain surface and top, with their tails reaching
into the nest.

By default a nest draws no new seed. Its per-class seeds continue the
root's seed sequence where the parent left off, so each nest class
draws exactly what a root run taken directly to the nest's resolution
would have drawn. Supplying an explicit seed starts an independent
stream and forfeits the identity with such a run. Output is
written as a NetCDF group inside the parent's file, as described in
the previous section.

\section{Complete specification of the LES-ensemble comparison}
\label{ssect:rcemip analysis}

This section specifies, in full, the analysis behind the comparison of
STEAM to the RCEMIP cloud-resolving ensemble
\citep[][]{wing2018,wing2020}. The intent is that the comparison could be
reproduced exactly from this description alone. The comparison is
against the elongated-channel (\texttt{RCE\_large300}) configurations of
nine models, used for the profile, distribution, and
correlation-dimension statistics; object size distributions, for which
the channel geometry is too narrow, are instead computed from a
wide-domain STEAM ensemble with no direct hydrodynamic counterpart, as
described below.

\subsection{Comparison data}
\label{ssect:rcemip data}

Nine models provide usable
three-dimensional snapshot output for the 300~K channel configuration:
SAM, CM1, three Met Office Unified Model variants (CASIM, RA1-T,
RA1-T-nocloud), SCALE, UCLA-CRM, and two ICON variants (LEM and NWP).
Domains are channels of approximately $6000\times 400$~km$^2$ at 3~km
horizontal spacing ($1984$--$2048$ cells long, $128$--$144$ cells wide),
with model-specific stretched vertical grids ($74$--$98$ levels to
$33$--$39.5$~km). Each model supplies three snapshots separated by ten
days (days 80, 90, and 100 of the simulation; days 93, 103, and 113 for
the UM variants; days 80, 90, and 99 for ICON-LEM). Two archived models
are excluded: ICON-AES, because its output contains neither a height
coordinate nor pressure, so moist static energy cannot be constructed;
and MESONH, because its archived three-dimensional specific humidity is
a documented data error in the RCEMIP archive (Known RCEMIP Bugs
document, Sect.~17: the field is ``much too small in the lower
troposphere'' by a height-dependent factor), while its column-integrated
water and remaining fields are physically normal --- the corruption is
in the archived field, not the simulation, but it contaminates every
vapor-dependent statistic used here.

All humidities are converted to mixing
ratios before any further computation. Most models archive specific
quantities (mass per unit moist air, CMOR names \texttt{hus},
\texttt{clw}, \texttt{cli}); these are converted as $r = q/(1-q)$, with
the vapor-only denominator (the neglected condensate contribution to
moist-air mass changes $r$ by ${\lesssim}2\%$ where condensate is
largest). SAM archives vapor as a mixing ratio directly. Cloud liquid
and ice are taken from \texttt{clw} and \texttt{cli} (UCLA names ice
\texttt{ice}); precipitating condensate is excluded everywhere, because
several models do not archive it.

Total water is $q_t = r_v + r_c + r_i$ and moist static energy is
$h = c_p T + g z + L_v r_v$ with $c_p = 1004$~J\,kg$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$,
$g = 9.81$~m\,s$^{-2}$, $L_v = 2.5\times 10^6$~J\,kg$^{-1}$ (STEAM's
constants, applied identically to every model).

Two archives require special
handling. (i)~The ICON files carry only a level \emph{index} (top-down)
as their vertical coordinate; physical height is recovered by inverting
the frozen moist static energy field distributed alongside them,
$z = (f_{\mathrm{mse}} - 1004.64\,T - 2500800\,q_v + 333700\,q_i)/9.80665$
(the constants of that archive, used only for this recovery), averaged
horizontally at one snapshot; the level-wise horizontal standard
deviation of the recovered height is required to be below 50~m.
(ii)~UCLA-CRM stores height as the fastest dimension and includes a
below-ground ghost level, which is dropped. Where a model archives no
pressure (SCALE, UCLA-CRM), the RCEMIP analytic-sounding surface
pressure of 1014.8~hPa is used to initialize STEAM; otherwise the
horizontal mean of the model's lowest-level pressure is used. All
reductions of single-precision fields use double-precision accumulators.

\subsection{STEAM configuration}
\label{ssect:rcemip steam config}

One deliberately untuned configuration is used throughout; nothing is
calibrated to any individual host. The spheroscale profile decreases
linearly from 100~m at the surface to 1~m at the domain top, with
turbulons isotropic below it
(\texttt{piecewise\_isotropic\_below\_spheroscale}); the horizontal Hurst
exponent is $H_h = 0.45$ and the vertical-anisotropy exponent
$H_z = 5/9$; turbulons use the Mexican-hat envelope; one scale class per
octave ($n_c = 1$). The turbulon flux component is active: flux
increments drawn from extremal ($\beta = -1$) L\'evy noise with $\alpha
= 1.8$ and noise amplitude $c = 0.142$, targeting a realized flux
intermittency of $C_1 = 0.05$ through the calibration $C_1 =
1.681\,c^{1.8}$ (box-coarse-grained $K(2)$ on the flux-only cascade,
re-fit 2026-07-28; \texttt{turbulon-analysis/calibration}).
% NOTE (Claude, 2026-08-04): that fit applied the interpolation
% compensation in-cascade to the flux increments, a convention since
% superseded -- the flux state now runs raw and the written flux is
% composed at the output. The mapping c -> C_1 of the composed output
% has not been re-fit; re-fit before final production numbers.

For the channel comparison STEAM runs on a $2048\times 128$ grid at
$\Delta x = 3$~km ($6144\times 384$~km$^2$, matching the host geometry
class) with outer scale $L = 96$~km, giving five scale classes (96, 48,
24, 12, 6~km). The domain is periodic; the 384~km cross-channel extent
holds four outer-scale tiles. The simulated depth is 20~km.

Each STEAM run is initialized from one host snapshot: the host's
horizontal-mean $h(z)$ and $q_t(z)$, interpolated to a uniform 50~m
profile grid, plus its surface pressure as above. The scalar bounds are
anchored to the prescribed 300~K sea surface temperature, which every
realization sees: $q_t \in [0,\ q_{\mathrm{sat}}(300\,\mathrm{K},
p_s)]$, since no parcel can acquire more vapor than saturated contact
with the sea surface provides, and $h \in [\min_z \langle h \rangle(z)
- c_p \Delta T_{\max},\ \max(c_p \cdot 300\,\mathrm{K} + L_v\,
q_{\mathrm{sat}}(300\,\mathrm{K}, p_s),\ \max_z \langle h
\rangle(z))]$, where the upper bound is the larger of the surface
saturation moist static energy and the mean-profile maximum (the $gz$
term places the stratospheric mean above any surface value), and the
lower bound allows a maximum temperature deficit of $\Delta T_{\max} =
10$~K below the coldest point of the mean profile. One realization is run per host
snapshot (three per model), with a distinct seed per snapshot, so host
and STEAM sample counts match by construction.

For size distributions and
large-domain fractal dimensions, additional STEAM runs use a square
$2048\times 2048$ grid at $\Delta x = 3$~km ($6144\times 6144$~km$^2$)
with outer scale $L = L_x/4 = 1536$~km, initialized from ICON-LEM
snapshot profiles with distinct seeds. These runs have no direct
hydrodynamic counterpart; they exist because the channel geometry
cannot constrain size-distribution exponents (Sect.~\ref{ssect:rcemip
fractal}), and the square geometry maximizes the usable lag range.

\subsection{Profile statistics and anomaly distributions}
\label{ssect:rcemip stats}

Per-level statistics are computed on each case's native vertical grid:
horizontal mean and variance of $h$ and $q_t$, and cloud fraction
defined as the areal fraction with non-precipitating condensate
$q_c + q_i > 0.01$~g\,kg$^{-1}$. For comparison across grids all
profiles are linearly interpolated to a common 100~m grid spanning
0--20~km (levels outside a model's native range are excluded, not
extrapolated), and snapshot-averaged.

Differences are presented as $\Delta = $ STEAM $-$ host, one curve per
model. The yardstick shading is built from the \emph{pairwise}
inter-host differences: for each level, all ordered pairs
host$_i$ $-$ host$_j$ ($i \neq j$; 72 pairs for nine models) are formed
from the snapshot-averaged profiles, and the shading shows their full
range and interquartile (25--75\%) range. Pairwise differences, rather
than deviations from the ensemble mean, are the like-for-like reference
for a pairwise quantity such as $\Delta$; deviations from the mean are
smaller by roughly $\sqrt{2}$ and would overstate STEAM's exceedances.

Anomaly probability densities are computed at the model level nearest
4~km and 10~km: at each level the horizontal mean of that snapshot is
subtracted, all snapshots of a case are pooled, and the pooled sample is
histogrammed in 150 equal-width bins spanning the pooled sample's full
range (no percentile capping, which would truncate the tails that are
precisely the point of the comparison), normalized to a density.

\subsection{Strip-nest multifractal diagnostics}
\label{ssect:strip diagnostics}

The scaling diagnostics for the multifractal-parameter appendix are
computed on thin nested strips refined from the square-domain runs
(Sect.~\ref{ssect:steam algorithm spec}, nested refinement): each strip
spans the full 6144~km $x$-extent (periodic, with the parent extent as
period), is 48~km wide in $y$, and is refined from $\Delta x = 3$~km to
$\Delta x = 375$~m --- three size classes below the parent's finest.

The fluctuation measure is the mean absolute Haar fluctuation: at lag
$\ell$, the mean of the field over the upper half of an $\ell$-wide
window minus the mean over the lower half, with $\hat{M}_q(\ell) =
\langle |\Delta\Phi_\ell|^q \rangle$ pooled over all window positions.
Horizontal windows run along $x$ at dyadic lags from $2\Delta x$ to a
quarter of the domain and wrap periodically; vertical windows run along
$z$ (uniform grid for a constant spheroscale) at dyadic lags up to
16~km, either over the full domain or with window centers restricted to
the 6--8~km mid-level band. The normalization test overlays the
field's vertical $\hat{M}_1(\ell)$ against the same operator applied to
the input mean profile: by the $C_{\Phi,L}$ construction
(Sect.~\ref{ssect:steam algorithm spec}, Normalization) the two must
agree at the vertical outer scale, in the same sense as the $\lambda$
calibration: a power law fitted to the field's fluctuations below the
outer scale and extrapolated to $k_{z,L}$ reproduces the profile
fluctuation there. The directly measured curve departs from the power
law near $k_{z,L}$, where the profile and the turbulence mix. Moment-scaling exponents $\xi(q)$
are least-squares slopes of $\ln \hat{M}_q$ against $\ln \ell$ over
lags up to 6~km (the nest's own classes plus one inherited octave), for
$q$ from 0.2 to 3; for the flux the moment scaling $K(q)$ is also
computed directly from one-dimensional box means along $x$ over the
same lags, $\langle \mathcal{F}_\lambda^q \rangle \sim
\lambda^{-K(q)}$. All accumulations are double precision.

\subsection{Optical-depth masks, correlation dimension, and size
distributions}
\label{ssect:rcemip fractal}

Cloud masks are built from vertically integrated optical depth computed
from the liquid and ice water paths with the SAM bulk-optics
relationships (as implemented in the \texttt{cloudyview} package);
precipitating species are excluded as above. A column is cloudy where
$\tau > 1$. The same masks and estimators are applied to the
square-domain runs, with correlation lags extended to one third of the
6144~km domain; at high $\tau>1$ cover the mask percolates into a
single spanning component, degrading the area-distribution estimate
(the correlation dimension is unaffected). Masks are binary arrays on
each case's native horizontal
grid; the three masks of a case are always analyzed together as one
ensemble, never averaged one-at-a-time.

The ensemble correlation dimension $D_2$ is computed with
\texttt{objscale} \citep{dewitt2026acp} using the sandbox estimator at
R\'enyi order $q = 2$ on the one-sided edge set of the masks --- the
Grassberger--Procaccia correlation integral --- with pair separations
binned between a minimum of two pixels (6~km) and a maximum of one-third
the narrow domain extent (${\approx}127$~km); sandbox centers are
subsampled by a factor of ten. The two-pixel channel minimum admits
pixel-scale discretization bias at the small end of the range and is the
price of retaining one decade of scaling in a 128-cell-wide mask. The
correlation integrals themselves are inspected for linearity in log--log
space before the fitted slope is reported as a dimension.

Object size distributions use the truncation-corrected estimator of
\citet{dewitt2024acp}: objects are 4-connected components of the mask;
areas (and summed perimeters) are binned logarithmically; bins in which
more than half the objects touch the domain boundary, or which hold
fewer than 30 objects, are excluded; the power-law exponent is the
least-squares slope of $\log_{10}$ counts against $\log_{10}$ size over
the surviving bins. Domains are periodic, but boundary-touching objects
are treated as truncated rather than wrapped --- a conservative choice
that discards, rather than reconnects, objects crossing the seam. For
the channel geometry the surviving range is only ${\approx}1.5$ decades
of area and the nine hosts' fitted exponents disagree by a factor of
four (0.6--2.3), so the channel ensemble does not constrain the exponent
at this domain size --- consistent with the finite-domain analysis of
\citet{dewitt2024acp}. Size distributions are therefore computed from
the wide-domain STEAM ensemble (all nine masks analyzed at once), along
with the individual fractal dimension (filled perimeter against filled
area) and the ensemble correlation dimension at the same estimator
settings, with sandbox centers subsampled by a factor of one hundred.
